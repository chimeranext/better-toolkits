# The Compression Ladder

The same five nodes at five resolutions. Resolution is a choice about the audience's attention budget, never a choice about which nodes survive.

## Contents

1. The direction rule

3. What gets cut first

## 1. The direction rule

**Write L4 first, then compress down.** Always.

Compression is subtraction and it is mechanical: you know what to remove because you can see what is load-bearing. Expansion is invention, and inventing upward from a one-liner produces padding, because you are generating support for a conclusion you have already committed to rather than testing whether the conclusion holds.

The practical consequence: when a user asks for a tagline, a bio or a one-line description, do not write a tagline. Build the argument at L4 in draft, silently if context is rich, then descend. A line that has an L4 underneath it can be defended in a meeting. A line without one collapses the first time someone asks why.

## 2. The five rungs

**L1. The line. About 25 words.**
I and B fused, R as a credential clause. P and E are implied by the choice of words.
Used for: bio, one-liner, DM, conference introduction, the answer to "what do you do".
Failure mode: describing the category instead of the position. If the line would fit a competitor, descend again from L4 with the logo-swap test running.

**L2. The paragraph. About 100 words.**
P in one sentence. I in one sentence. B in one clause. One proof from E. One number from R.
Used for: cold email, About paragraph, LinkedIn post, the opening of a talk, a slide of context.
Failure mode: spending 60 of the 100 words on P. The tension is the cheapest thing to write and the least differentiating, so it gets the least space at this rung.

**L3. The page. About 500 words.**
All five nodes, visible or invisible, one page.
Used for: sponsorship one-pager, internal memo, executive summary, press note, grant abstract, proposal cover.
Failure mode: turning E into a feature list. At this rung Execution gets roughly 180 words and they should be the six surfaces in one line each, not fifteen features.

**L4. The full argument. 1,500 to 2,500 words, or 10 to 14 slides, or 20 minutes.**
All five nodes with evidence, alternatives considered, and the turn written deliberately.
Used for: seed deck, keynote, essay, proposal, launch narrative.
This is the canonical rung. Everything else is derived from it.

**L5. The board. Full case board plus appendix.**
All five plus methodology, the discarded routes, the data appendix, and the credits.
Used for: award submission, investment memo, institutional application, board paper.
Failure mode: the appendix eating the argument. The board is still a film. The appendix is what the jury reads only if the film worked.

## 3. What gets cut first

Descending from L4, cut in this order. Stop when you hit the word budget.

1. Alternatives considered and the discard log
2. Execution surfaces beyond the top two
3. Secondary and tertiary result tiers
4. Problem evidence, keeping the assertion
5. The named consensus in P, keeping the tension
6. The Execution proof, keeping one credential
7. The Problem, which becomes implied by the Insight

Note what is never on the list. **The Insight and the Big Idea survive every rung, including L1**, where the whole line is those two nodes fused. If a compression forces a choice between keeping a number and keeping the Insight, keep the Insight. The number proves you are real. The Insight is why anyone should care that you are real.

## 4. Worked descent

Same argument, five rungs. The subject is a proof-of-presence infrastructure company for live events.

**L4 (summarized here, written in full in practice).** Sponsorship is settled on numbers nobody can verify; the industry's fraudulent attendance figures exist because the story is due before the proof is possible; the company sells the proof layer rather than the counting; it ships as an attendance primitive that sponsors, venues and organizers all read from; and the first cohort of events using it renewed at a rate the category does not see.

**L3 (500 words).** P: sponsors buy on numbers they cannot check, and the gap has a price, expressed as declining renewal. I: the fraud is a timing problem, not an honesty problem. B: proof of presence. E: the primitive, the sponsor-facing report, the venue integration, the pricing that charges per verified attendee rather than per event. R: renewal rate, verified attendees processed, and the first sponsor who moved budget on the basis of the report.

**L2 (100 words).** Sponsors settle every deal on attendance numbers nobody can verify, and the industry knows it. The fraud is not an honesty problem, it is a timing problem: the story is due before the proof is possible. So we built the proof layer instead of a better counter. Proof of presence, issued at the moment someone is actually there, readable by the sponsor, the venue and the organizer at once. First cohort: [N] verified attendees across [M] events, and the first sponsor to reallocate budget on the strength of a report rather than a promise.

**L1 (25 words).** We built proof of presence for live events, because sponsorship fraud is a timing problem, not an honesty one. [N] verified attendees so far.

The descent stays honest because every rung is a subtraction from the same L4. Written in the other direction, L1 would have produced a tagline and then two hundred words of retrofitted justification.
